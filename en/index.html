HEALTHY AGING / MOVEMENT BEHAVIORS / DATA SCIENCE

I started as a research assistant in my third year of university, went on to a PhD, worked in Busan, South Korea, and returned to Taiwan. My initial motivation was practical: a job, an income, and a willingness to learn whatever I didn’t know. Somewhere between cleaning data, running analyses, and writing papers, I began to think research might actually suit me. So here I am.

I study how older adults move, sit, and sleep throughout the day, and how these behaviors relate to muscle strength, cognition, and healthy aging. Wearable devices and statistical analysis help me ask more specific questions about the familiar advice to move more. (Eventually, even the time of day became a research question.)

Many mentors, colleagues, and collaborating teams have helped me along the way. For every paper on which I am first or co-first author, I have personally written the manuscript from start to finish, handled every round of revision, and written the point-by-point responses to reviewers through to the end. (I typed the thanks for the reviewers’ valuable comments. I also typed the several pages that followed.)

MY ACADEMIC JOURNEY

My academic journey: remembering the denominator

I often joke that academia is for people who have run out of options. Explaining how I got here takes a little more space than that.

On a CV, it looks straightforward: bachelor’s degree, PhD, research professor in Busan, postdoctoral researcher back in Taiwan, then assistant professor. In between came reading papers, cleaning data, rejected submissions, silence after interviews, and discovering another hurdle just when things seemed to be working out. (CVs list job titles. They rarely list how long you had to hang in there.)

Getting here took my own effort, but also introductions from my advisor, support from collaborators, access to data, and opportunities that happened to come along. All of these mattered. When sharing my experience, I try to remember those who worked just as hard but did not end up on a faculty list: the denominator behind the success stories.

I wrote about this in Becoming an Assistant Professor at 30: A Conversation with the Denominator. It covers the path I took, its costs, the approaches others might find useful, and the circumstances that are harder to reproduce. Hard work does not guarantee a payoff. It is also fine to be tired, or to choose another path.
